Most brides walk into a salon or a maker's studio with a list of things they want and no list of things they need to know. The questions to ask before buying a wedding dress cover what you are buying, the fabric, the fittings, the timeline, what is included and what happens on the day. The first list gets longer with every appointment. The second stays empty, and then a problem shows up after the deposit has cleared: the fabric arrives a different shade, the bustle turns out to cost extra, the third fitting was never part of the deal.

The twelve questions below are the ones to ask before paying anything. They apply to a salon rail, to a designer who cuts to your measurements, and to a maker found online. Some will feel awkward. Ask them anyway. A good salon or maker answers them easily, because they have been asked before.

## What should you ask about the dress itself?

### 1. What exactly am I buying?

There are three different things sold under the name "wedding dress", and they carry different rights and risks.

**Off the rack.** You buy the sample you tried on, or a new copy in a standard size, and it is altered to fit. Delivery is fast. What you saw is what you get, minus whatever alterations can do.

**Ordered from a maker.** The salon takes your measurements, orders the closest standard size from the designer, and alters it when it arrives. This is the usual salon model. The dress you receive is new, in your nearest size, and it will need work.

**Made to measure.** The dress is cut to your body from the start. There is usually a toile or muslin stage, more fittings, and a longer timeline. Fewer surprises at the end, more decisions along the way.

Ask which of these you are getting, and get the answer written on the order form. The word "custom" is used loosely and can mean any of the three.

### 2. Is the sample fabric the fabric I will receive?

Samples are made from the same fabric as the production dress in most cases, but not all. A sample may be an earlier season's silk that has since been replaced with a polyester version, or a lace that the mill no longer makes. Ask directly: will my dress be made from this exact fabric, from this supplier, in this shade? If the answer is anything softer than yes, ask to see a swatch of what will actually be used. Our [fabrics guide](/guides/wedding-dress-fabrics-guide/) explains what the differences feel like on the body.

### 3. What happens if the delivered dress differs from the sample?

Dye lots vary. Lace patterns get discontinued. A designer changes the lining mid-season. Ask what the salon or maker will do if the dress arrives and is not what you ordered: remake it, discount it, or tell you it is within tolerance. Ask what "within tolerance" means before you need to know.

### 4. What can and cannot be altered?

Every dress has fixed points. On most, the point where a skirt flares cannot be moved, a lace bodice cannot be taken in at the bust without cutting through the pattern, and a beaded neckline cannot be reshaped. Ask the fitter, not the salesperson, to walk through the dress and say what is adjustable. If you plan to change the neckline, add sleeves or shorten a train, ask now whether that is possible and who will do it. The flare point in particular is fixed on nearly every dress.

## What should you ask about the process?

### 5. How many fittings are included?

Two is common for ordered dresses, three or four for made to measure. Ask how many are included in the price you have been quoted, what happens if you need another one, and how long each takes. A number is a better answer than "as many as you need", which tends to mean something different once the diary fills up.

### 6. What is the exact timeline, with buffer?

Ask for dates, not durations: when the order goes in, when the dress is expected, when the first fitting happens, and when you collect. Then ask what buffer sits between the expected delivery and your first fitting. A dress that arrives two weeks before the wedding with no buffer leaves no room for a remake. Six to eight weeks between delivery and the wedding is a comfortable buffer; two is not.

### 7. Who does the alterations?

An in-house seamstress, an outside tailor the salon recommends, or someone you find yourself? Each is workable, but each has consequences. In-house alterations mean the salon owns the result. An outside tailor means the salon can say the problem was introduced after the dress left them. Ask who is responsible if an alteration goes wrong.

### 8. What should I bring to each fitting?

The answer tells you how organised the salon is. You should hear: the shoes you will wear, the underwear you will wear, and one person whose opinion you trust. If nobody mentions shoes, ask when you need to have chosen them. The [fittings guide](/guides/wedding-dress-fittings-guide/) covers what to check at each stage.

## What is included in the price?

### 9. What counts as an extra?

This is where quotes drift. Ask whether the price includes each of the following.

| Item | Often included | Often extra |
|---|---|---|
| Petticoat or underskirt | Ball gowns from some makers | Most salons |
| Sewn-in cups | Made to measure | Ordered dresses |
| Bustle for the train | Rarely | Almost always, done at alterations |
| Garment bag | Usually | Sometimes a branded box instead |
| Steaming before collection | Usually | Sometimes |
| Cleaning after the wedding | Almost never | Always |

Ask for a written list of what the quoted price covers. Then ask what the bustle will cost separately, because a train that cannot be bustled is a train you will carry all evening.

### 10. What are the cancellation and change terms?

Deposits on ordered and made-to-measure dresses are usually non-refundable, because the maker starts work at once. Ask what happens if the wedding is postponed, if you change your mind about the design after ordering, and whether any part of the deposit is recoverable in either case. Ask how late you can change details such as sleeves, lining colour or train length without a new charge.

## The day itself

### 11. Who dresses me?

A dress with a lace-up back, a row of thirty covered buttons or a five-point bustle needs someone who knows how it closes. Ask whether the salon offers a dressing service on the day, and if not, ask whether your helper can attend the final fitting to learn the closure and the bustle. Film it. This is a small question that saves a difficult half-hour.

### 12. What happens to the dress afterwards?

Ask whether the salon offers cleaning and preservation, or can recommend someone who does, and what the timeframe is. Stains set quickly. Knowing the plan before the wedding means the dress does not sit in a bag for three months. Our [cleaning and preservation guide](/guides/cleaning-and-preserving-wedding-dress/) explains what to look for.

## Why write it all down?

Take a notebook to every appointment and write the answers as you hear them, with the name of the person who gave them. Memory is unreliable after four salons and a glass of something. Then ask for the important answers in writing: what you are buying, the fabric, the number of fittings, the dates, the list of inclusions and the cancellation terms. An email summarising the conversation, sent to the salon and acknowledged, is enough.

None of this is about distrust. It is about the fact that a wedding dress is bought once, months ahead, from people who sell many of them and remember fewer of the details than you will. Written agreements protect both sides, and a professional will not object to them.
